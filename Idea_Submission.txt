BeaconLock: AI-Assisted Virtual Camera Simulator for FSOC Coarse Pointing, Acquisition & Tracking


==========================================================================================
ABSTRACT / SUMMARY  (max 10,000 characters)
==========================================================================================

Free-Space Optical Communication (FSOC) sends data on a narrow laser beam through the air or through space. It offers very high data rates, needs no radio-spectrum licence and is hard to intercept, which makes it attractive for ground-to-drone, ground-to-aircraft, inter-satellite and ground-to-satellite links, and for secure defence and disaster-recovery networks. Its main difficulty is pointing. The beam is only a fraction of a milliradian wide, so the two terminals must find each other and stay aligned while both may be moving, the platform vibrates, the atmosphere makes the signal twinkle and wander, clouds block it, and other bright lights appear in the sky. This job is called Pointing, Acquisition and Tracking (PAT). It starts with a coarse stage: a wide-field camera on a pan-tilt gimbal must search the sky, detect the partner terminal's beacon, confirm that it really is the beacon, and keep it close enough to the image centre that a fine-steering mirror can take over.

Testing coarse PAT algorithms on real hardware is expensive, slow, weather-dependent and hard to repeat, and there is no ground truth for where the beacon actually was. BeaconLock is a complete, open, reproducible software test bench for this coarse stage. It is a virtual pan-tilt camera tracking simulator that closes the full PAT loop in software and measures its own performance against exact ground truth.

A synthetic sky is rendered at 30 frames per second on a 640 x 480 sensor with an 8 x 6 degree field of view (1 pixel = 0.22 mrad). It contains a beacon that blinks with a known 4 Hz "code", decoy lights (steady, or blinking at wrong rates), hundreds of background stars, moving clouds, sun glare, short glints, beacon dropouts, atmospheric turbulence (scintillation, beam wander, blur and heat shimmer), platform vibration (structural tones plus random jitter), and realistic sensor effects (shot and read noise, hot pixels, random pixel hits, motion blur). The beacon moves along lissajous, circular, linear or random-walk paths. The virtual gimbal has real slew-rate and acceleration limits. Every disturbance has an on/off switch and a strength multiplier, so conditions can be varied from a clear sky to an extreme worst case.

The processing chain is the one a real terminal would run. Bright candidates are found with an adaptive threshold, connected components and sub-pixel centroids, plus a more sensitive window around the predicted beacon position. The key innovation is identity-aware tracking. In a single frame every bright dot looks the same, so BeaconLock follows every candidate over time and decides which one is the beacon from how it behaves. Two scorers are combined. The first is a classical blink test that measures the signal-to-noise ratio of the 4 Hz component of the candidate's brightness. The second is BeaconNet, a compact convolutional neural network (62,818 parameters) that looks at a 0.8-second clip of 24 image patches of each candidate and outputs the probability that it is the beacon. BeaconNet runs through OpenCV's DNN module from an ONNX file, so no deep-learning framework is needed at run time. Evidence is accumulated over up to 3 seconds before the tracker accepts a candidate (probability at least 0.7) or rejects it as a decoy and ignores it for a while. A lock that later looks wrong is dropped automatically. Tracking uses a constant-velocity Kalman filter in world angles, so the camera's own motion is removed, with statistical gating and a four-state PAT sequence: SEARCH, ACQUIRING (verification), TRACKING and COASTING (predicting through short occlusions). A PID controller with target-velocity feed-forward and anti-windup steers the gimbal. When the beacon is lost, a predictive spiral search centred on where the beacon should now be takes over, with a full-sky raster only as a last resort.

On held-out validation scenes, BeaconNet reaches 93.3 % accuracy and ROC-AUC 0.976, against 0.903 for the classical blink test alone. In a closed-loop evaluation over 40 random scenes, the hybrid identifier acquired the true beacon in 92 % of runs, against 62 % for the conventional "lock onto the brightest dot" approach. It cut runs with sustained false locks from 42 % to 8 %, with a median lock retention of 100 % after acquisition. Processing cost is about 8 ms per frame on an ordinary laptop CPU, well inside the 33 ms real-time budget. Six fixed, reproducible scenarios (clear sky, strong turbulence, occlusion, decoys, fast target, worst case) were each run over 10 seeds and reported with 95 % confidence intervals. They show clearly where the system is strong and where it struggles, for example heavy occlusion and extreme combined disturbances.

Every run is logged frame by frame against ground truth. BeaconLock reports acquisition time, mean, RMS and maximum pointing error in pixels and milliradians, lock retention, false-lock frames, time ready for fine-pointing handover, frame rate and processing time. It automatically produces a self-contained interactive HTML report with rotatable 3D views of the beacon path and camera pointing, a printable PDF report, a per-frame CSV file and JSON/text summaries. Each run's random seed is saved, so any run can be replayed exactly.

The software is easy to use for students, researchers and engineers. A dark "mission control" desktop application shows the live camera feed with detections, beacon scores and the tracking window, zoomable to 8x. It also shows a live 3D view of the terminal, sky dome, field of view (coloured by tracker state), search path, beacon, decoys and clouds, together with live plots, performance cards, live sliders for turbulence, vibration and noise, and a choice of scenario and identification method. The whole application can be driven from the keyboard. A lightweight OpenCV viewer and a headless batch mode support fast experiments, and a multi-seed evaluation tool produces report-ready statistics. The project ships as a Windows executable that needs no installation, and it includes an 18-page illustrated user manual, a technical report, a dataset generator and training scripts for retraining the neural network.

BeaconLock turns coarse PAT from a hardware-only, hard-to-measure problem into one that can be simulated, measured and compared honestly. It lets teams develop, tune and compare pointing, acquisition, tracking and identification algorithms before building hardware. It gives Indian FSOC, drone, satellite and defence programmes a low-cost, fully indigenous test bench, and it gives students a hands-on way to learn PAT.

1. BACKGROUND AND PROBLEM UNDERSTANDING
------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------

1.1 Why FSOC matters
Free-Space Optical Communication (FSOC) carries data on a modulated laser beam through the atmosphere or through space instead of through a cable or radio waves. Compared with radio-frequency links it offers:
 - very high bandwidth (gigabits to terabits per second);
 - no spectrum licensing, since optical frequencies are unregulated;
 - a very narrow beam, which makes the link hard to detect, intercept or jam, an important property for defence and strategic communication;
 - small, light, low-power terminals, suited to drones, aircraft and small satellites;
 - rapid deployment for disaster recovery, last-mile connectivity and temporary networks where laying fibre is impossible.
These properties make FSOC central to future satellite constellations, inter-satellite links, deep-space communication, secure military links, high-altitude platforms and 5G/6G backhaul, all areas where India's space and defence programmes are active.

1.2 The pointing problem
The strength of FSOC, a very narrow beam, is also its biggest challenge. A typical optical link must be pointed to within tens to hundreds of microradians. At that scale, the vibration of a vehicle, the sway of a mast or the motion of a drone is enough to break the link. Both terminals must therefore run a Pointing, Acquisition and Tracking (PAT) system, usually in stages:
 1. Open-loop pointing: the terminal aims at where the partner should be, using GPS, ephemeris or navigation data. This prior is typically several degrees off.
 2. Coarse acquisition and tracking: a wide-field camera on a pan-tilt gimbal searches around that prior, detects the partner's beacon, verifies it and steers the gimbal to keep it near the image centre (boresight).
 3. Fine tracking: once the beacon sits inside a small handover zone, a fast-steering mirror and a quadrant detector or fine sensor take over for micro-radian accuracy.
This project addresses stage 2, the coarse stage. It is where the system first finds its partner, and it is where most real-world failures start.

1.3 What makes coarse tracking hard
The coarse camera sees a small, dim, moving spot among many sources of confusion:
 - Atmospheric turbulence makes the beacon twinkle (scintillation), jump around (angle-of-arrival wander), blur and shimmer.
 - Platform vibration shakes the line of sight, through structural resonances and random jitter.
 - Clouds dim or completely block the beacon, sometimes for seconds.
 - Other lights appear: other aircraft, bright stars, reflections (glints), street lights and the sun or moon (glare). Many are as bright as the beacon.
 - Sensor noise, hot pixels and cosmic-ray-like pixel hits create false detections.
 - The target moves and manoeuvres, while the gimbal has limited slew rate and acceleration.
 - The initial pointing error can be several degrees, so the beacon is often not in the field of view at the start.
A tracker that simply follows the brightest dot will often lock onto the wrong light. A tracker without prediction loses the beacon at every cloud, and a controller without feed-forward lags behind a fast target.

1.4 Why a simulator is needed
Developing coarse PAT on real hardware is:
 - Expensive: optical terminals, gimbals, drones and test ranges cost a lot.
 - Weather-dependent and non-repeatable: the same test can never be run twice under identical turbulence, clouds and target motion, so algorithm A cannot be compared fairly with algorithm B.
 - Hard to measure: in the field there is no ground truth for where the beacon really was, so pointing error, false locks and acquisition time cannot be measured precisely.
 - Slow: every change needs a field trial.
There is therefore a clear need for a software test bench that reproduces the effects that make coarse tracking hard, runs the complete closed PAT loop, and measures performance exactly and repeatably. BeaconLock is that test bench.


2. PROPOSED SOLUTION: BEACONLOCK
------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------

BeaconLock is a Virtual Camera Tracking Simulator for the coarse stage of FSOC PAT. It is a complete closed-loop system in which:
 1. A virtual world contains a blinking optical beacon, decoy lights, stars and clouds, all moving on configurable trajectories.
 2. A virtual pan-tilt camera renders exactly what a real coarse-tracking camera would see, including turbulence, vibration, clouds, glare, glints and sensor noise.
 3. A detection, identification and tracking pipeline finds candidates, decides which one is the beacon (using AI and signal processing), and estimates its position and velocity.
 4. A controller moves the virtual gimbal: it follows the beacon when tracking and runs an intelligent search pattern when the beacon is lost.
 5. A performance monitor compares everything against exact ground truth and produces detailed reports.
The whole loop runs at 30 frames per second in real time, or much faster in batch mode, on an ordinary laptop, with no special hardware.

Processing loop (one iteration per frame):
  Scene update -> Render camera view -> Apply disturbances -> Detect bright blobs
  -> Identify the beacon (AI + blink test) -> Kalman tracker -> PID controller -> Move gimbal
                                              -> Performance monitor (ground truth comparison)


3. DETAILED TECHNICAL APPROACH
------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------

3.1 Virtual world and targets
 - The sky is an azimuth/elevation region (default: azimuth +/-30 degrees, elevation +/-20 degrees) with a configurable background brightness and around 600 background stars. The stars are dimmer than the detection floor but add realistic clutter.
 - Targets are rendered as Gaussian spots with configurable brightness and size, and with per-source motion blur that depends on their motion relative to the camera during the exposure.
 - Trajectories: static, linear, lissajous, circular and random walk (with a turn-rate parameter), so both smooth and manoeuvring targets can be modelled.
 - Beacon code: the designated beacon blinks at a known rate (default 4 Hz, 70 % duty cycle, dimming to 30 % rather than switching fully off). Decoys can be steady lights, or can blink at the wrong rate, including rates close to 4 Hz or at its harmonics.
 - Random scenes: by default every run draws a new scene. The beacon path is lissajous, circular or random walk at 0.8 to 5 degrees per second. The scene has 2 to 5 decoys, each steady or blinking at a wrong rate, random turbulence and vibration strengths, clouds with 50 % probability, and a random initial pointing error of 3 to 10 degrees, which models a GPS/ephemeris prior. The seed of every run is recorded, so any run can be replayed exactly.

3.2 Virtual pan-tilt camera
 - Resolution 640 x 480 pixels and field of view 8 x 6 degrees, so one pixel is 0.0125 degrees (0.22 mrad).
 - The gimbal has a slew-rate limit (25 degrees per second per axis) and an acceleration limit (80 degrees per second squared per axis), so the controller faces real actuator constraints.
 - World-to-pixel and pixel-to-world projection use the camera pose at the moment of capture.
 - All parameters are set in a single documented configuration file.

3.3 Disturbance models
Each disturbance has an enabled switch and a strength multiplier (0 = off, 1 = nominal, 2 = twice as strong, and so on), so every effect can be studied on its own or in combination.
 - Platform vibration: sinusoidal structural modes (default 7 Hz and 23 Hz) plus broadband correlated random jitter, applied to the line of sight.
 - Atmospheric turbulence: scintillation (log-normal intensity fluctuation with a correlation time), angle-of-arrival wander of the spot, extra blur (a wider point-spread function), and heat-shimmer image distortion (a smoothly varying warp field over the image).
 - Clouds: several moving occluders with random size and optical depth, drifting with the wind. They attenuate the beacon (transmission = exp(-depth)) and add their own glow.
 - Dropouts: complete blockages of the beacon for 0.5 to 2 seconds at random intervals, to test coasting and re-acquisition.
 - Glints: short, bright false flashes at random positions inside the field of view.
 - Glare: a bright extended source (sun or moon) that raises the background locally.
 - Sensor: shot noise proportional to the square root of the signal, read noise, fixed hot pixels, random single-pixel hits every frame, and exposure-dependent motion blur.

3.4 Candidate detection
 - Optional pre-smoothing, then an adaptive threshold (image median plus k times a robust noise estimate, with a minimum floor), so detection adapts to glare, clouds and noise levels.
 - Connected-component labelling with area limits to reject single-pixel hits and large extended regions.
 - Intensity-weighted sub-pixel centroids for precise positions.
 - A tracking window (region of interest) around the tracker's predicted beacon position uses a lower threshold. A faded or twinkling beacon is still detected where it is expected, without raising false alarms elsewhere in the image.

3.5 Beacon identification: the core innovation
In a single frame every light is just a spot, so the beacon cannot be told apart from a decoy of similar brightness. BeaconLock therefore identifies the beacon by its behaviour over time.
 a) Candidate tracklets: every detection, not just the one being tracked, is associated from frame to frame in world coordinates, so camera motion does not break the association. For each candidate a small 24 x 24 pixel image patch is stored every frame, which builds a short video clip per candidate.
 b) Classical blink test: aperture photometry on the patches gives a brightness time series. A single-bin Fourier transform (Goertzel algorithm) measures how much of the signal power lies at the beacon's code frequency (4 Hz) compared with the rest. This is transparent and explainable, and it is good at rejecting decoys that blink at the wrong rate.
 c) BeaconNet (AI): a compact convolutional neural network takes the last 24 patches (0.8 s) as a 24-channel image. It has three convolution blocks (32, 64 and 64 filters, with batch normalisation, ReLU and pooling), global average pooling, dropout and a two-class output, giving P(beacon). It has only 62,818 parameters and takes a fraction of a millisecond per candidate on a CPU. The input is normalised (background subtracted, divided by the peak), so the network learns the temporal pattern of blinking, fading and vanishing independently of absolute brightness or cloud dimming. It is trained entirely on simulator data, with labelled clips of beacons, steady decoys, wrong-rate and near-rate decoys, stars and noise under a wide range of turbulence strengths. It is exported to ONNX and run with OpenCV's DNN module, so the application does not need PyTorch or any deep-learning framework at run time.
 d) Hybrid scoring (default): the average of the CNN and blink-test probabilities. The CNN is better at recognising the beacon under heavy turbulence, and the blink test is better at rejecting decoys. Together they produced the fewest false identifications.
 e) Evidence accumulation: per-clip scores are converted to log-odds and averaged over up to 90 frames (3 s of memory). A candidate is confirmed as the beacon only after at least 24 clips (about 1.6 s of history, enough to tell 2 Hz from 4 Hz) and an averaged score of at least 0.7. It is rejected at 0.2 or below and then ignored (blacklisted) for 4 s. If a locked target's score later averages below 0.35 for a full second, the lock is dropped as a false lock and the search resumes.
 f) Selectable methods: hybrid, CNN only, blink test only, or none (lock onto the brightest dot, the conventional baseline). Methods can be compared fairly under identical conditions.

3.6 Tracking and the PAT state machine
 - A constant-velocity Kalman filter estimates azimuth, elevation and their rates in world angular coordinates. Each detection is converted to world angles using the camera pose at capture time, so the tracker sees the true target motion rather than the image motion caused by the gimbal.
 - Measurements are gated statistically (Mahalanobis distance, chi-square gate for 2 degrees of freedom at about 99.7 %), with a small minimum gate so very close detections are always accepted.
 - The state machine implements the real PAT acquisition sequence:
     SEARCH     no beacon: the controller scans the sky.
     ACQUIRING  a candidate has been found and is being verified (several hits and enough identification evidence are needed).
     TRACKING   a confirmed lock. Short gaps such as the blink "off" phase or brief fades are bridged without leaving this state.
     COASTING   the beacon is temporarily lost (for example behind a cloud). The filter predicts its motion and returns to TRACKING on re-detection, or gives up and returns to SEARCH when the prediction becomes too uncertain or too much time has passed.
 - Rejected decoys are blacklisted, so the tracker does not keep re-acquiring the same wrong light.

3.7 Pointing control and search
 - Tracking control: a PID controller on the pointing error (default gains Kp = 5, Ki = 0.5, Kd = 0.05) plus target-velocity feed-forward from the Kalman filter, so the gimbal moves with the target instead of lagging behind it. It includes integral anti-windup and respects the gimbal's rate and acceleration limits.
 - Search: when the beacon is lost, a predictive spiral scan starts from the last estimate and drifts along the target's last known velocity, because the beacon has kept moving. The arm spacing is a fraction of the field of view, so no gaps are left. After several local spirals, a full-sky raster pass is used as a last resort.
 - Initial acquisition starts from the open-loop pointing prior, as a real terminal would.

3.8 Performance monitoring and metrics
Because the simulator knows the true beacon position in every frame, it measures performance exactly:
 - acquisition time: the time to the first confirmed lock on the true beacon;
 - pointing and tracking error (mean, RMS, maximum) in pixels and milliradians;
 - lock retention: the share of time locked after the first lock, reported both overall and while the beacon was visible;
 - number of lock losses and false-lock frames (locked on something that is not the beacon);
 - share of locked time ready for fine-pointing handover (beacon within a 10-pixel radius of boresight);
 - share of time the beacon was in the field of view or occluded;
 - achieved frame rate, processing time per frame and processing throughput;
 - identification statistics: candidates rejected and false locks dropped.

3.9 Reports and logs
Every run automatically writes a folder containing:
 - report.html: a self-contained, offline, interactive report with run information, a performance table, a rotatable 3D view of the beacon trajectory and camera line of sight on the sky dome, a 3D azimuth/elevation/time view, and charts of pointing error with lock periods, the tracker-state timeline and processing time;
 - report.pdf: the same report in printable form;
 - frames.csv: per-frame states, true and estimated positions, errors and timing;
 - summary.json and summary.txt: all summary metrics.
Each report records the seed and settings, so any result can be reproduced.


4. USER INTERFACES AND TOOLS
------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------

4.1 Desktop application (mission-control dashboard)
 - Live camera feed showing all detections with colour-coded beacon scores (green = beacon-like, red = rejected, yellow = undecided), the tracked candidate, the Kalman estimate with a velocity arrow, the tracking window, the boresight crosshair and the handover circle. Optional ground-truth circles show where the beacon and decoys really are. The feed can be zoomed 2x, 4x or 8x around the tracked beacon, which makes the blinking and noise of a few-pixel target visible.
 - 3D situational view: the ground terminal on its pan-tilt mount, the sky dome with the simulated sky region, the camera's field-of-view pyramid coloured by tracker state, the history of where the camera has looked (so the search spiral becomes visible), the beacon with its trail, decoys, clouds and the tracker's estimate. It can be rotated and zoomed with the mouse or keyboard, and it has four preset views.
 - Live plots of pointing error (with the handover limit and lock periods) and of the beacon score of the followed candidate (with the accept and reject thresholds).
 - Performance cards: simulated time, acquisition time, lock retention (colour-coded), pointing error in px and mrad, frame rate and processing time, and identification statistics.
 - Controls: start, pause, restart the same scene, new random scene, scenario selection, seed entry for exact replay, identification method, number of decoys, run length and simulation speed (0.5x to maximum).
 - Live disturbance sliders for turbulence, vibration and sensor noise that act instantly during a run, so users can see the tracker cope or fail.
 - Full keyboard control, a legend popup, a shortcut help screen, one-click report saving and a button that opens the reports folder.
 - The layout adapts to screen sizes from 1366 x 768 laptops to large monitors.

4.2 Lightweight viewer and batch mode
 - A minimal OpenCV viewer with a text overlay (state, time, FPS, error, acquisition time, retention, identification) and a world minimap.
 - A headless mode that runs a whole simulation as fast as the computer allows and writes only the report, for large experiments.
 - Command-line options for the scenario file, duration, seed and output name.

4.3 Evaluation and research tools
 - Multi-seed evaluation: runs many seeds per configuration and writes summary tables with 95 % confidence intervals, breakdowns by turbulence, decoys, speed, clouds and path type, and box plots. It saves after every run, so an interrupted evaluation resumes where it stopped.
 - A method-comparison tool runs the none, blink, CNN and hybrid methods side by side on the same scenarios.
 - The dataset generator produces labelled training clips from the simulator. The training script trains BeaconNet, reports accuracy, precision, recall, F1 and ROC-AUC against the classical baseline, draws training curves and confusion matrices, and exports to ONNX. New models go into a separate candidate folder, so they never overwrite the production model before validation.

4.4 Deployment
 - A Windows executable built with PyInstaller, in two variants: a folder version with fast start-up and a single-file version. It needs no Python installation.
 - A built-in self-test mode (--selftest) runs a short simulation, exercises the CNN and report generation, and writes PASSED or FAILED to a text file, so a packaged build can be checked without opening the GUI.
 - An 18-page illustrated user manual and a technical report are included.

4.5 Technology stack
Python 3; NumPy for numerical simulation; OpenCV for image processing, rendering and CNN inference (DNN module with ONNX); PySide6 (Qt) for the desktop application; pyqtgraph with OpenGL for the live plots and the 3D view; PyYAML for configuration; Plotly.js (embedded, offline) for the interactive HTML reports; ReportLab and Matplotlib for the PDF reports; PyTorch and scikit-learn only for offline training of the network; PyInstaller for packaging. Every component is open source and runs on an ordinary CPU.


5. SCENARIOS AND TEST CASES
------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------
Besides random scenes, six fixed and reproducible scenarios are provided. A scenario file lists only what differs from the default configuration, so new ones are easy to create.
 1. Clear sky: no disturbances, the ideal baseline.
 2. Strong turbulence: heavy turbulence, vibration and sensor noise.
 3. Occlusion: dense clouds plus beacon dropouts, testing coasting and re-acquisition.
 4. Decoys: several lights of similar brightness, testing target identification.
 5. Fast target: a fast, manoeuvring beacon, testing controller and filter agility.
 6. Worst case: everything at once.

------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------

 - Classical blink test: ROC-AUC 0.903.
 - The network is especially better at recognising the beacon under strong turbulence, where the blink signal is distorted by scintillation.

7. INNOVATION AND UNIQUENESS
------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------
 1. Identity-aware tracking. Most simple trackers follow the brightest spot. BeaconLock follows all candidates and identifies the beacon by its temporal code, which prevents most false locks on aircraft, stars, glints and other lights.
 2. Explainable hybrid AI. A lightweight CNN is combined with a transparent signal-processing test, with the evidence accumulated and thresholded, so decisions are both accurate and explainable. Its scores are shown live on every candidate.
 3. Framework-free AI deployment. The network runs through OpenCV DNN from an ONNX file, so the same model can move to embedded platforms (Jetson, Raspberry Pi and similar) without PyTorch.
 4. Simulation-trained learning. The simulator generates unlimited labelled training data with exact ground truth, removing the need for costly field data collection, and the network can be retrained for any new beacon code or sensor.
 5. A complete PAT sequence, not only a tracker: open-loop prior, predictive spiral search, verification, tracking, coasting through occlusion, re-acquisition and a handover criterion for the fine stage.
 6. Exact, honest measurement. Ground-truth-based metrics, multi-seed evaluation with confidence intervals, and exact replay by seed support fair comparison of algorithms.
 7. Every effect can be switched and scaled. Each disturbance can be turned on or off or scaled, including live during a run, so the influence of each effect can be studied on its own.
 8. Usable by non-experts. The mission-control GUI, 3D visualisation, keyboard control, one-click reports, a Windows executable and an illustrated manual make it usable in classrooms as well as labs.


8. FEASIBILITY
------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------
 - Technical feasibility is shown: the full system is implemented, tested and packaged, runs in real time on a normal laptop CPU, and has been evaluated over 180 closed-loop runs.
 - Cost: it uses only free and open-source software on commodity hardware, with no licences and no special equipment.
 - Maintainability: the code is modular (world, camera, disturbances, detector, identification, tracker, controller, metrics and GUI are separate modules), all parameters live in one documented configuration file, and the simulation core does not depend on the GUI.
 - Extensibility: new trajectories, disturbances, identification methods, filters or controllers can be added as modules and compared immediately with the existing evaluation tools.
 - Path to hardware: the processing pipeline (detection, identification, Kalman tracking, PID control) is the same one a real terminal runs. Replacing the virtual camera with a real camera driver and the virtual gimbal with a real pan-tilt unit is a well-defined step, and the tuned parameters and trained model carry over as a starting point.

It reduces dependence on imported tools and expensive field trials, in line with the Atmanirbhar Bharat goals.

 - For AI development: a synthetic data engine for training and validating learned target-identification models with perfect labels.

11. LIMITATIONS AND RISK MITIGATION
------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------
Known limitations are documented openly:
 - Geometry uses a small-angle projection over a limited sky region. This suits the 8-degree coarse camera; a spherical projection and full gimbal kinematics are planned for wide-field or near-zenith use.
 - The constant-velocity Kalman filter lags hard manoeuvres. An interacting-multiple-model filter is the planned remedy.
 - Identification needs about 1.6 s of evidence. A sequential probability ratio test or a faster beacon code could shorten acquisition.
 - Turbulence is set by parameters (pixels, log-intensity) rather than derived from physical quantities such as Cn-squared, range and wavelength. Linking it to a link budget would make the simulator quantitatively predictive for a specific link.
 - Only the coarse stage is simulated; the fine-pointing stage is represented by a handover radius.
 - Evaluation used 10 to 40 seeds per configuration, so confidence intervals are reported rather than single numbers.
Mitigation: the modular design isolates each of these, the evaluation tools make every improvement measurable, and new models are kept separate until validated.


12. FUTURE SCOPE
------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------
 1. Hardware-in-the-loop: connect a real camera and pan-tilt unit, and validate against the simulator with an LED beacon on a drone.
 2. A physics-based atmosphere: turbulence derived from Cn-squared profiles, range, wavelength and aperture, with day and night sky backgrounds and sun-glint geometry.
 3. The fine-pointing stage: a fast-steering-mirror and quadrant-detector model, closing the full PAT loop down to microradians.
 4. Two-terminal simulation: both terminals running PAT and tracking each other simultaneously, with realistic relative motion (satellite passes, UAV flight paths).
 5. Advanced estimation and control: interacting-multiple-model filters, model-predictive gimbal control, adaptive search patterns.
 6. Stronger identification: temporal transformer or recurrent models, coded beacons (pseudo-random blink sequences) for multiple-terminal networks, and on-device deployment on embedded AI boards.
 7. Link-level metrics: converting pointing error into received power, bit-error rate and link availability.
 8. Web and cloud version: browser-based access and large-scale parallel evaluations for teams.

14. CONCLUSION
------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------
BeaconLock gives the coarse stage of FSOC Pointing, Acquisition and Tracking a complete, realistic and measurable software environment. It reproduces the conditions that make coarse tracking hard: turbulence, vibration, clouds, decoys, sensor noise, fast targets and large initial pointing errors. It runs the full PAT sequence in closed loop and measures the result exactly against ground truth. Its identity-aware hybrid AI identification raises acquisition success from 62 % to 92 % in random scenes and cuts false-lock runs from 42 % to 8 %, while staying well within real-time limits on an ordinary CPU. Because it is open, reproducible, easy to use and ready to connect to real hardware, BeaconLock can speed up the development of optical communication terminals, reduce dependence on costly field trials, and serve as a teaching and benchmarking platform for India's growing optical communication, space and defence ecosystem.
